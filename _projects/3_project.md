*This page describes a research proposal currently in preparation for submission to the AHRC. It is not yet funded, and this page is not linked from the site while the submission is in progress.*

## Context

Public discourse has grown increasingly sceptical of the value of studying the humanities: that they don't address real-world problems, don't teach workplace-useful skills, and rest on subjective opinion rather than scientific fact. The rise of generative AI has sharpened this scepticism further — if an LLM can summarise a novel, a philosophical argument, or a theological text in seconds, why study literature, history, divinity, or philosophy at all?

## The Idea

This project argues that these views miss what the humanities actually train: the capacity to hold and work creatively with **multiplicity of meaning**. At the most basic level, multiplicity of meaning is linguistic ambiguity — the fact that words and sentences can carry more than one sense, which human language processing normally resolves instantly and invisibly, collapsing many possible interpretations into one. But several humanities disciplines deliberately reverse this process: instead of hiding ambiguity, they bring it into conscious view and treat the reader or listener as a co-creator of meaning rather than a passive decoder.

We see this across the humanities in different guises: in linguistics, where the study of ambiguity is used to reveal the structure of grammar itself; in literature, through practices like erasure poetry and participatory texts that hand the reader an active role in constructing meaning; in philosophy of language, through conceptual engineering, which asks not just how words are used but how they should be reshaped; and in sociolinguistics, through phenomena like plausible deniability, where meaning is actively negotiated between speakers and listeners rather than simply transmitted.

## Aims

1. **Genres of multiplicity of meaning** — map how different humanities subdisciplines engage with ambiguity, and what concepts of language, meaning, and creativity underlie each approach.
2. **The cognitive value of engaging with ambiguity** — develop an "alternative interpretations task" to measure lay people's ability to consciously discover and articulate multiple meanings, and relate this to established measures of creativity and open-mindedness.
3. **Testing across populations** — explore how this ability and experience varies by age (adolescents vs. older adults) and neurotype (neurotypical vs. autistic/ADHD/dyslexic participants), drawing on developmental psychology's account of adolescence and later life as critical periods for social cognition and theory of mind.

## Why It Matters

If successful, this would be the first systematic demonstration that these practices span the humanities while taking discipline-specific forms, and the first psychological operationalisation of the ability to consciously detect and work with multiple meanings — with applications in education, and in exploring whether engaging with ambiguity is something that benefits wellbeing and cognitive flexibility across the lifespan.
